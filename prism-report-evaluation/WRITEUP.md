## The aim

After UNI2, I wanted to go one step further.

```
UNI2   →  numbers in, LABEL out
PRISM  →  slide in, LANGUAGE out
```

PRISM can look at a whole slide and write a free-text pathology report.

It can also classify subtypes with **no training at all**.

So the question:

> How good are these vision-language pathology models — and where do they fail?

**The real aim, again, was the honest one:**

A model that writes a fluent, confident-sounding report is exactly the thing that's dangerous when wrong.

It *reads* authoritatively.

So:

```
1. Generate real reports
2. Check them against ground truth
3. Separate what it gets right from what it makes up
```

Step 3 is the point.

## The pipeline

```
              Raw whole-slide image
              (TCGA-56-8308, lung)
                        |
              Tile into patches
              + tissue/quality filter
                        |
              VIRCHOW encoder (frozen)
              2,560 numbers per tile
                        |
              PRISM (Perceiver aggregator)
              one slide representation
                        |
            +-----------+-----------+
            |                       |
      Text decoder            Zero-shot
       (BioGPT)            compare to prompts
            |                       |
    "Poorly differentiated    [adeno 0.0005,
     SCC ... left lower       squamous 0.9995]
     lobe lung"
```

Compare this with the UNI2 pipeline. Two differences jump out.

**First: I trained nothing.**

Every box is a frozen model I merely ran.

**Second — and this matters more: there is no interpretability tail.**

In UNI2, the attention branch continued *past* the score:

```
UNI2:   score → heatmap → crop tiles → morphology check
PRISM:  score → (nothing)
```

The flow just stops at the output.

**That missing tail IS the limitation.**

PRISM won't tell me which tiles produced the word "keratinizing."

So there's nothing to map back onto the tissue.

The diagram makes the gap visible.

## Why Virchow, not UNI2

PRISM doesn't take a slide image directly.

It also doesn't take my UNI2 embeddings.

It takes tile embeddings from a *different* encoder:

| | UNI2-h | Virchow |
|---|---|---|
| Used in | project 1 | project 2 |
| Numbers per tile | 1,536 | **2,560** |
| Pre-computed for TCGA? | **yes** (Mahmood Lab published them) | **no** — for my slide |

That last row is the whole reason Phase 2 was hard.

```
UNI2 project   →  someone had published the embeddings  →  I streamed them
PRISM project  →  nobody had, for my slide              →  I ran Virchow myself
```

The heavy step I dodged in the first project had to be done by hand in this one.

That means: raw slide, tiling, a GPU.

## The two-stage ladder

Worth having clear, because it recurs everywhere.

```
one tile   ──[ tile encoder ]──►   one vector    (UNI2 1,536 / Virchow 2,560)
                                        │
                    ...×5,355 of these──┤
                                        ▼
whole slide ──[ slide encoder ]──►  one vector    (PRISM: 1,280)
```

**Tile encoder** (UNI2, Virchow) — looks at a 256-px patch. Has no idea a slide exists.

**Slide encoder** — never looks at pixels at all. Only sees tile-vectors. Fuses them into one.

**You have already built one.**

Your attention-MIL *is* a slide encoder. Hand-rolled and small.

Your mean-pool baseline is one too — the crudest possible: no learning, just average.

PRISM's slide encoder is the same idea, industrialised:

```
called a Perceiver
pretrained on 587,000 slides
outputs 1,280 numbers
```

You saw its output directly:

```
Slide embedding shape: torch.Size([1, 1280])       ← the slide, as one vector
Latents shape:         torch.Size([1, 512, 1280])  ← richer version, for the text decoder
```

**The honest framing of your two projects:**

```
UNI2 project   →  someone else's tile encoder
                  MY OWN slide encoder (built + trained)

PRISM project  →  someone else's tile encoder
                  someone else's slide encoder
                  my effort went into EVALUATING the result
```

Same ladder. Different rung of the work.

## Reading the shapes

This triple recurs constantly:

torch.Size([1, 12137, 2560])

My first run used **1,500** — about an eighth as much.

My clean run's 5,355 brought me far closer to the density they demonstrate on.

Small extra piece of evidence that the first run was undersampled — independent of the blood problem.

## Phase 1 — the shortcut

PRISM ships with a ready-made example.

The Virchow embeddings for one breast slide are bundled with the model.

So I could run the **entire** PRISM pipeline without running Virchow at all.

**Why do this first:**

Same reasoning as the whole UNI2 project.

```
Prove the machinery works on pre-computed embeddings
   ↓
THEN tackle the hard part
```

**The result:**

```
Report      "Moderately differentiated invasive ductal carcinoma
             with micropapillary features in breast tissue."

Zero-shot   [lobular 0.0013, ductal 0.9987]

Ground truth (GDC)  "Infiltrating duct carcinoma, NOS"
```

Infiltrating duct carcinoma = invasive ductal carcinoma. Same thing, older name.

**So the core diagnosis was correct.**

But look at what else it volunteered:

```
"moderately differentiated"     ← a grade
"micropapillary features"       ← a named architectural variant
```

The ground truth says **NOS** — *not otherwise specified*. No special subtype recorded.

So those two specifics **can't be confirmed or refuted** from the record.

**First lesson:**

```
the headline can be right
while the extra detail is unverifiable
```

*(It's a breast slide, incidentally, because Paige chose the example — not me. The barcode B6 is TCGA's breast cohort, the GDC record is a breast diagnosis, and the bundled prompts are ductal vs lobular — the classic breast question. Phase 1 was there to validate machinery; I had no choice of organ.)*

## Phase 2 — my own slide

I took a lung slide I already knew from the UNI2 project.

```
TCGA-56-8308
confirmed squamous cell carcinoma
I'd already seen its heatmap and its keratinising morphology
```

**The steps:**

```
1. Download the raw WSI from GDC
2. Verify Virchow outputs the 2,560 format PRISM wants  ← on ONE test tile
3. Tile the slide, filter background
4. Run each tile through Virchow
5. Feed the embeddings to PRISM
```

Step 2 mattered. Debug one thing at a time — better to catch a format mismatch on a single dummy tile than after twenty minutes of embedding.

**The result:**

```
Zero-shot   [adeno 0.00017, squamous 0.99983]     ← CORRECT

Report      "Moderately differentiated keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma
             with perineural invasion IN LYMPH NODE."
                                       ↑
                                   that's wrong
```

It's a lung primary. Not a lymph-node metastasis.

**Confirmed two ways:**

```
project_id  = TCGA-LUSC        (lung squamous cohort)
barcode     = TCGA-56-8308-01Z
                            ↑
                   "01" = primary solid tumour
```

**So:**

```
cancer type   →  exactly right
anatomy       →  confidently wrong
```

That's the kind of error that matters. Fluent, specific, and false.

## The part I'm most pleased with

I asked **why** it said "lymph node."

Then I looked at the tiles I'd actually fed it.

**What I found:**

```
dominated by blood and debris
```

And the first run had simply taken the **first 1,500 tiles from the top of the slide**.

So: an unrepresentative, biased slice. Not a fair sample of the tumour.

**The hypothesis:**

> The error was caused by poor input. Not by an irreparable flaw in the model.

That's a testable claim. So I tested it.

## Option B — engineering a better filter

The old filter was crude:

```
keep anything that isn't white
   ↓
blood is not white
   ↓
fields of red blood cells sailed through
```

The fix uses **colour**, not just darkness:

```
blood      is RED     →  high red, low blue
nuclei     are BLUE   →  haematoxylin stains them blue-purple
```

**The rule:**

```
reject  if red-dominated pixels > 20% of the tile
require blue/purple (nuclei) > 22%
require not-white (tissue)   > 45%
```

**Why 20% and not stricter?**

Because you asked the right question:

> *Could some tumours be very vascular — is that why there's so much blood?*

Yes. Tumours can be genuinely vascular. Squamous carcinomas haemorrhage.

The blood isn't always artefact — it can be real biology.

So the threshold was set **deliberately generously**:

```
keep    tumour tiles that CONTAIN some blood
reject  tiles that ARE mostly blood
```

We're not filtering blood because blood is meaningless.

We're prioritising tiles rich in diagnostic epithelium.

**Also changed:**

```
sample the WHOLE slide     (not just the top)
checkpoint to Drive every 200 tiles   (so a Colab timeout costs seconds, not the run)
```

**The result of filtering:**

```
~22,300 grid positions on the slide
filter rejected ~70% of non-white tiles
   ↓
5,355 clean tiles kept    (~24% of the full grid)
```

vs 1,500 blood-heavy ones before.

## The finding

Same slide. Same model. **Only the input differed.**

```
MESSY input (1,500 blood-heavy, top-biased)
   "Moderately differentiated keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma
    with perineural invasion IN LYMPH NODE."

CLEAN input (5,355 filtered, whole-slide)
   "Poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma
    with lymphovascular invasion IN LEFT LOWER LOBE LUNG."
```

**The site error is gone.**

```
"in lymph node"           →  WRONG
"in left lower lobe lung" →  CORRECT, and specifically so
```

Zero-shot stayed squamous (0.9995). The diagnosis never wobbled.

**So:**

```
I hypothesised a cause
I engineered a fix grounded in tissue biology
I re-ran
The fix worked
```

That's the strongest part of the project.

---

## The subtler finding

Other things changed too, and not all obviously for the better:

| | messy input | clean input |
|---|---|---|
| Grade | moderately differentiated | **poorly** differentiated |
| Keratinizing? | yes | **dropped** |
| Invasion | perineural | **lymphovascular** |
| Site | lymph node ❌ | left lower lobe lung ✅ |
| Diagnosis | squamous ✅ | squamous ✅ |

**Why did "keratinizing" disappear?**

We can't know for certain — PRISM gives no explanation. But the likely story:

```
messy run  →  1,500 tiles from the TOP of the slide
           →  if keratinizing areas were concentrated there,
              they made up a LARGE fraction of what PRISM saw

clean run  →  5,355 tiles across the WHOLE slide
           →  those same areas are now a small slice
           →  averaged out
```

The feature didn't disappear from the tumour. It got **diluted** in a more representative sample.

**And the grade change is linked:**

```
keratinization = a marker of differentiation

well/moderately differentiated  →  keratinizes
poorly differentiated           →  often doesn't
```

So the model moved from "moderate + keratinizing" to "poor" (which implies non-keratinizing).

It didn't randomly drop a word. The two changes **hang together** biologically.

**Then I looked at the clean tiles.**

Predominantly solid, non-keratinizing tumour.

Which suggests the **clean run's description was the more accurate one** — and the earlier "keratinizing" was an artefact of the biased sample.

**So better input improved the report on multiple axes.**

Not just the one I targeted.

*(Caveat: tumours are heterogeneous. Both descriptions could be "true" of different regions. The clean run reflects the majority pattern. And this is a thumbnail-level read — full-res would confirm.)*

**The honest caution that comes out of it:**

```
the headline diagnosis  →  stable
the fine print          →  wobbles with the sample
```

Grade, invasion type, keratinization — all sensitive to which tiles the model sees.

Don't trust a single generated report's grade without knowing what tissue it was based on.

## How zero-shot works

The counterintuitive part. Worth slowing down on.

> How can a model score a slide 99.95% "squamous" when nobody trained it on that question?

**Normally you would train:**

```
LUAD images
LUSC images
     ↓
train a classifier
```

That stage is **completely skipped**.

**Instead you just ask:**

```
Compare my slide to
  "squamous cell carcinoma"
vs
  "adenocarcinoma"
```

The model already knows what those phrases mean. It learned them during its original image–text training.

It has **zero examples** of your specific task.

Hence **zero-shot**.

---

### Why it works: one shared space

PRISM was trained on:

```
587,000 slides
        PAIRED WITH
195,000 clinical reports
```

That pairing forces images and words into **one space**:

```
        a shared space
   ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │   "squamous cell         │
   │    carcinoma"  ●         │
   │            ●  ← my slide │
   │                          │
   │   "adenocarcinoma" ●     │
   └──────────────────────────┘
```

During training, squamous slides kept appearing alongside reports saying "squamous cell carcinoma."

So the model learned to place that *image* and that *phrase* near each other.

The word and the picture end up as neighbours.

---

### So the classification is just distance

```
1. Turn my slide into a point         →  [1,280 numbers]
2. Turn "squamous cell carcinoma"
   into a point                       →  [1,280 numbers]
3. Turn "adenocarcinoma" into a point →  [1,280 numbers]
4. Which phrase-point is closer?      →  squamous, by a mile
5. Convert distances to probabilities →  [0.0005, 0.9995]
```

No training. No labels. No gradient updates.

Just: embed the words, embed the image, measure which is nearer.

---

### Where the probabilities come from

The similarities go through a **softmax** — which converts scores into probabilities that sum to 100%.

*(Same spirit as the sigmoid in logistic regression, but for multiple classes.)*

There's a detail worth knowing:

```
the model multiplies the similarities by a learned TEMPERATURE
before the softmax
```

Temperature is a **sharpening dial**.

It's what turns a modest similarity gap into a lopsided 99.95%.

**Which means:**

```
99.95%  ≠  "the slide screamed squamous"
99.95%  =  "a moderate gap, amplified by a dial someone else set"
```

Another reason to check the record rather than trust the number.

---

### The critical limitation

The probabilities **always sum to 100%**.

So suppose the slide is actually **normal lung**.

You ask:

```
Option 1   squamous
Option 2   adenocarcinoma
```

PRISM still measures both distances. One of them **must** be closer.

It may confidently answer "squamous" — for a slide with no cancer in it.

**Because it's answering the question you asked:**

```
✓  "Which of these labels is more similar?"
✗  "Does this slide belong to either category?"
```

Those are different questions.

**The model cannot say "neither."**

So 99.95% means:

> *of the two phrases you gave me, this one is much closer*

Not:

> *this is certainly squamous*

Very different claims.

A real workflow would need an explicit "none of the above" or out-of-distribution step.

### The two projects, side by side

| UNI2 + Attention-MIL | PRISM |
|---|---|
| Needs labelled training data | No labelled data needed |
| I trained a classifier | No classifier trained |
| Boundary learned from my dataset | Boundary comes from image–text similarity |
| Learns LUAD vs LUSC from examples | Uses prompts: "LUAD", "LUSC" |
| Supervised learning | Zero-shot inference |

**In plain terms:**

```
UNI2 + MIL:  "Learn from these labelled examples
              where the LUAD/LUSC boundary is."

PRISM:       "I already understand the relationship between
              pathology images and diagnostic terms.
              Tell me the labels you care about —
              I'll compare your slide to those concepts."
```

**And the interesting part:**

My UNI2 pipeline learned the distinction from ~300 labelled slides.

PRISM reached the same call with **zero** task-specific training — because its image–text pretraining had *already* organised pathology images and diagnostic language into a shared space.

**But hold the caveat:**

```
UNI2   →  0.95 AUROC over ~75 test slides
PRISM  →  1 correct call on 1 slide
```

Those aren't comparable. One correct answer isn't a performance measurement.

**Safe version:**

> "On the single slide I tested, PRISM's zero-shot call matched what my trained model would have said — with no task-specific training. I'd need many more slides to claim comparable performance."

## The training details — why there aren't any

In the UNI2 project this section held epochs, a loss curve, a confusion matrix.

Here it's near-empty. The reason is a real distinction between the two projects.

**I trained nothing.**

Every step was **inference** — running frozen models and reading the output.

| | UNI2 project | PRISM project |
|---|---|---|
| Did I train a model? | Yes (attention-MIL) | **No** |
| Epochs | 20 | **none** |
| Loss curve | 0.69 → 0.017 | **none** |
| Train/test split | ~225 / ~75, by patient | **none** |
| Confusion matrix | [[9 1] [1 9]] | **none** |
| What I did | designed + trained a classifier | ran frozen models, *evaluated* the output |

**Why no train/test split?**

A split exists to stop a model memorising its training data.

Nothing was fitted. So there's nothing to leak.

**Why no AUROC?**

AUROC needs many slides with known labels.

This is n = 1. There's no meaningful curve to compute.

**So what IS the result?**

```
not a performance number
but a FINDING about how the model behaves and where it fails
```

A different kind of result. Describe it as such — don't dress it up with metrics it doesn't have.

## How the results were validated

Since I didn't verify the code myself, this matters.

Nothing rests on trusting the pipeline blindly:

```
The diagnoses      →  checked against INDEPENDENT ground truth
                      (GDC record + the slide barcode)
                      NOT against the model's own confidence

The input quality  →  checked by eye. I looked at the tiles.

The fix            →  checked by re-running and comparing.
                      The site claim demonstrably changed.
```

**What could NOT be verified:**

```
"moderately differentiated"    ← not in TCGA's fields
"perineural invasion"          ← field is blank ('--')
"keratinizing"                 ← not recorded
```

TCGA's `pathology_details` table is **almost entirely unpopulated** for this case.

So those claims can be neither confirmed nor refuted from the record.

Only reading the full slide could settle them.

**That absence is itself a finding:**

PRISM generated a specific, confident, detailed-sounding report for a case where the official structured record contains almost none of those details.

You often *can't* fully fact-check the output.

Which is exactly why a human in the loop matters.

**And the scale caveat, stated plainly:**

```
n = 1
```

A well-verified case study. Not a general claim about PRISM's reliability.

## Glossary

**Multimodal AI** — works across more than one *type* of data at once. PRISM is image + text. UNI2 was image only.

**Vision-language model (VLM)** — trained on paired images and text, so it can connect what it sees to words. This is what lets PRISM write reports and classify from prompts.

**Virchow** — the tile encoder PRISM is built on. 2,560 numbers per tile. Gated.

**PRISM** — the slide-level multimodal model. Aggregates Virchow tile embeddings, generates text, does zero-shot. Gated. ~558M parameters.

**Tile encoder vs slide model** — two levels. Tile encoder (Virchow/UNI2) describes small patches. Slide model (PRISM) combines them into a whole-slide understanding.

**Perceiver** — the architecture PRISM uses to aggregate a variable number of tiles into a fixed-size slide representation. Its version of my attention-pooling step.

**Class token / patch tokens** — a vision transformer outputs one summary vector (class token) plus one per sub-region (patch tokens). PRISM wants: [class token] + [mean of patch tokens] = 2,560 numbers.

**Report generation (captioning)** — the model writes free text describing the slide, word by word, via a text decoder. PRISM uses **BioGPT**, a biomedical language model.

**Beam search** (`num_beams=5`) — explores several candidate phrasings and keeps the best. More coherent than always grabbing the single most likely next word.

**do_sample=False** — makes generation deterministic. Same input → same report every time.

**Zero-shot classification** — classifying with no training, by comparing the slide's embedding to the *text* of candidate labels. Limitation: always picks the nearer of the phrases you supply. Can't answer "neither."

**Shared embedding space** — the arrangement PRISM learned by training on slides paired with reports, where an image and the words describing it land near each other. What makes zero-shot possible.

**Softmax** — converts similarity scores into probabilities that sum to 100%.

**Temperature** — a learned sharpening dial applied before the softmax. Why the outputs look so confident.

**Prompt (pos/neg)** — the text descriptions given to zero-shot to compare against.

**Inference (vs training)** — *training* = the model learns and updates. *Inference* = you run a finished model and read the output. Everything here was inference.

**Tokenize / untokenize** — converting text to the numbers a language model uses, and back. `untokenize` turns PRISM's numeric output into readable English.

**Autocast / mixed precision / inference_mode** — efficiency wrappers. Run on the GPU in half-precision, don't track gradients. Nothing conceptual.

**Gated repo** — a model requiring approval to download. Virchow and PRISM both need an institutional-email HuggingFace account.

**Checkpointing / resumable pipeline** — saving progress to disk periodically, so a crash resumes instead of restarting. The fix for Colab resets.

**Tissue / quality filter** — the rule deciding which tiles to keep. The improved one used colour: reject red-dominated (blood), require blue/purple (nuclei). The key lever in Option B.

**Perineural / lymphovascular invasion** — pathology findings PRISM asserted. Not recorded in TCGA's structured fields. Hence "unverifiable."

**GDC portal** — public source of the raw slides and the ground-truth diagnoses used to verify PRISM's output.

## FAQ

**How is this different from the UNI2 project?**

UNI2 = numbers in, label out. I trained a classifier. PRISM = slide in, *text* out. Nothing trained.

**Does PRISM use the UNI2 embeddings I already had?**

No. It needs **Virchow** embeddings (2,560-dim). A different encoder.

**Why did I have to run Virchow myself when UNI2's embeddings were pre-computed?**

Because nobody had released Virchow embeddings for my slide. The Mahmood Lab published UNI2 features for TCGA; no equivalent existed here. So the heavy step I dodged in project 1 had to be done by hand in project 2.

**Was 2,560 the number per tile, or the total?**

**Per tile.** `[1, 12137, 2560]` = 1 slide, 12,137 tiles, 2,560 numbers each. The total for that slide is ~31 million.

**Was the Phase 1 slide really breast?**

Yes. Four independent lines agree: the barcode (`B6` = TCGA breast cohort), the GDC record ("infiltrating duct carcinoma"), the bundled prompts (ductal vs lobular — the classic breast question), and PRISM's own output ("in breast tissue").

**Why breast in Phase 1 and lung in Phase 2?**

Phase 1 used Paige's bundled example — I had no choice of organ. It was there to validate machinery. Phase 2 was my own slide, and I chose lung because I already knew its ground truth and morphology from the UNI2 project.

**What does zero-shot mean here?**

PRISM classifies by comparing the slide to the *words* "squamous cell carcinoma" vs "adenocarcinoma." No training. It scored 99.98% squamous — matching ground truth.

**How can it be 99.95% confident with no training?**

Images and text share one space, so it measures which phrase-point sits nearer the slide-point. And a learned temperature sharpens a moderate gap into a lopsided number. It always picks the nearer of *whatever phrases I supply* — it cannot answer "neither." So 99.95% means "much closer to this phrase than that one." Not "certainly squamous."

**Why was "in lymph node" an error?**

The slide is a lung primary (confirmed by TCGA's records and the `-01Z` barcode), not a nodal metastasis. Right cancer, wrong anatomy.

**How did cleaning the tiles fix it?**

The first run fed PRISM a blood-heavy, top-of-slide sample. A colour-based filter plus whole-slide sampling gave it 5,355 clean, representative tiles. On that input, the site corrected to "left lower lobe lung."

**Could the blood be real, not artefact?**

Yes — tumours can be vascular, and squamous carcinomas haemorrhage. So the filter rejects only *blood-dominated* tiles, keeping tumour tiles that contain some blood. A deliberate biological choice, not a blanket "blood is noise."

**Why did "keratinizing" disappear on the clean run?**

Most likely diluted: it was over-represented in the biased top-of-slide sample and averaged out in the larger whole-slide one. Reviewing the clean tiles suggests the tumour is predominantly non-keratinizing — and "poorly differentiated" is biologically consistent with that. Can't be proven without full-slide review. PRISM gives no per-word evidence.

**Can I see which tiles PRISM used for the report?**

**No.** Unlike the UNI2 attention model, PRISM doesn't expose per-tile importance. It generates from the whole aggregated slide. A real limitation.

**Would running all ~23,000 tiles be better?**

No — and it's worth knowing why. **Memory** isn't the bottleneck (each embedding ~10 KB; 23k ≈ 230 MB; tiles processed one at a time). **Time** scales with tiles embedded. But most of those positions are background glass — wasted compute — and it would re-introduce the blood and debris I removed. More tiles ≠ better. *Cleaner* tiles is what helped.

**Did I train anything?**

No. Every step was inference on frozen models. So: no epochs, no loss curve, no train/test split, no confusion matrix. Not an omission — nothing was fitted, so those concepts don't apply.

**Then what was the validation, if there's no test set?**

External ground truth — the GDC record and the tissue itself. Not a held-out split. A split guards against memorising training data; with no training, there's nothing to leak.

**How many slides is this based on?**

One own slide, plus one provided example. **n = 1.** A well-verified case study. Not a general claim. Running the LUAD slide would move it toward a pattern.

**Why did the Colab session keep dying?**

Free Colab times out on inactivity and wipes variables *and* installed packages. PRISM and Virchow are heavy — big models, long installs — so recovery was slow. The fix: checkpoint to Drive, and save results the moment you get them.
